# Feature-Level Rubric: Design Notes

This document records the thought process behind the [feature-level rubric guideline](../../guidelines/contributors/feature-level-rubric-guidelines.md) (FR 2335): the problem it answers, the design choices considered, the applicability-matrix method the companion mechanics guideline will define, and the questions still open. It is development background, not specification content, and parts of it describe analysis that is ongoing or still to be stress tested. Where a column level appears here, it is the output of reasoned analysis under the draft rubric, not a proposal to change any column. Companion document: [blind-rater evidence](feature-level-rubric-blind-rater-evidence.md), which records how the guideline text itself was tested.

The guideline is scoped to the Mandatory and Conditional levels. Some of the reasoning recorded here reaches further than that scope, because it was developed against a fuller draft; where that is the case it is marked as deferred rather than removed, so the analysis survives for the revision that picks the deferred questions up.

## The Problem the Rubric Answers

A FOCUS column's feature level has historically been an output, not a choice. The level falls out of how the presence requirement happens to be written: an unconditional MUST reads as Mandatory, a conditional MUST as Conditional, a SHOULD as Recommended, a MAY as Optional. Nothing decided what the requirement should be in the first place, so each column was leveled case by case, and leveling debates recurred because there was no rule to appeal to.

Two recurring failures motivated the rubric:

* **Presence and nullability argued as one question.** "Make it Mandatory, generators without it can leave it null or fill it from another column" answers a presence question with a nullability answer. The concrete in-spec trace is a Mandatory cost column whose definition resolves non-applicable operating models by substituting another column's value, so the presence question never gets asked.
* **Derivation without a leveling rule.** Columns linked by derivation were leveled with nothing connecting them, so a source column could be dragged to the level of something computed from it, or a derived column could constrain its own source. The rubric's answer is the directional derivation principle: a derived column can be no more present than its sources, a derived column's absence never pulls a source down, and each source is leveled by its own inputs. The related question, whether a column already computable from other columns belongs in the schema at all, is an admission question this revision defers.

Underneath both, Mandatory had become the default rather than a high bar, so columns that only some operating models can produce were mandated anyway, and the generators that could not produce them faced a choice between padding values and publishing dead columns. That is an adoption barrier for SaaS, AI, and internal-IT data generators, and removing it is the rubric's primary goal.

## Why the Operating Model is the Leveling Basis

The rubric levels by operating model, never by technology category, for three reasons:

* **Categories classify generators, not schemas.** A category (cloud, SaaS, PaaS, data center, AI) describes who the data generator is. It has no normative force about what their billing data contains. The characteristics that actually decide whether a column applies (does the model include regions, commitment discounts, virtual currency) cut across categories.
* **Category names churn.** What are called technology categories today were called scopes earlier, and may be renamed again. A rubric keyed to category names would inherit that churn; one keyed to operating model characteristics does not.
* **Category rules leak into levels.** Any rule of the form "SaaS does not populate X" hides a category inside a normative statement and caps generators by their label. The rubric's phrasing guardrail exists to keep that construction out of the specification: levels and defaults are never written as category restrictions, and a generator may meet any operating model Condition regardless of how it is classified.

The operating model Conditions vocabulary (the specification's Conditions section, each entry reading "the operating model includes X") is what makes this workable: applicability variance is expressed as a named, self-asserted, verifiable state instead of a category assumption.

## Variance Gates and Derivability

The Conditions section describes its entries as verifiable states that determine whether a data element is present, which reads as a test of whether a concept exists. Six of the twenty-five shipped Conditions do something else. Three gate on two values differing ([IncludesPricingBillingCurrencyDifferences](../../specification/conditions/includespricingbillingcurrencydifferences.md), [IncludesBillingPaymentCurrencyDifferences](../../specification/conditions/includesbillingpaymentcurrencydifferences.md), [IncludesAggregationLevelCurrencyDifferences](../../specification/conditions/includesaggregationlevelcurrencydifferences.md)) and three on more than one value occurring ([IncludesMultipleBillingAccountTypes](../../specification/conditions/includesmultiplebillingaccounttypes.md), [IncludesMultiplePricingCategories](../../specification/conditions/includesmultiplepricingcategories.md), [IncludesMultipleSubAccountTypes](../../specification/conditions/includesmultiplesubaccounttypes.md)). Between them they gate twelve columns across three of the four datasets, so this is a pattern in the specification rather than a stray entry.

Applicability alone returns Mandatory for every one of them, because the underlying concept is universal. Every model prices in some currency; every model has a billing account with some type. So the applicability test as originally drafted returned Mandatory against a shipped Conditional in each case, and the guideline needed a rule rather than a list of exceptions.

Derivability was the candidate rule, and the guideline does not adopt it. The reasoning ran this way: the working group has held that a column derivable from other columns should not be published unless it earns its place, so when a model prices and bills in one currency, [PricingCurrency](../../specification/datasets/cost_and_usage/columns/pricingcurrency.md) equals [BillingCurrency](../../specification/datasets/cost_and_usage/columns/billingcurrency.md) on every row and the column restates a Mandatory one, while a single billing account type is a free-form string recoverable from no other column. That would have made the first gate admissible and the second not.

What defeated it is that the equality is a commercial arrangement rather than a property of the schema. Whether a [*data generator*](../../specification/metadata/data_generator/data_generator_overview.md) prices and bills in the same currency is its own decision, it can change, and the Condition evaluating false is an assertion about that decision rather than a derivation from the data. A rule that reads recoverability off such an assertion treats a business choice as a schema fact.

The guideline therefore states the simpler rule: applicability returns Mandatory, a constant is a truthful value, and a gate on variance does not lower the level. Every one of the six Conditions above produces a constant when it evaluates false, so none of them is admissible as a presence gate, and all twelve columns they gate return Mandatory under the rubric. Reconciling those twelve against their shipped levels is a leveling question for a later release, not something this revision decides.

Two things follow that are worth stating plainly. The guideline defers derivability as an admission test, the question of whether a calculable column belongs in the schema at all. The rule here is the same intuition applied one level down, to whether a column already in the schema is present in a given dataset instance. They are different questions and the deferral stands, but the closeness is why the connection is drawn here rather than left implicit.

The second is that the specification does not currently apply this consistently, independent of the rubric. [ServiceProviderName](../../specification/datasets/cost_and_usage/columns/serviceprovidername.md) is Mandatory and repeats on every row of a single-provider dataset. BillingCurrency is Mandatory and repeats in a single-currency dataset. [BillingAccountType](../../specification/datasets/cost_and_usage/columns/billingaccounttype.md) is Conditional precisely because it would repeat. Under the rule stated above the first two are correct and the third is not, and [PricingCategory](../../specification/datasets/cost_and_usage/columns/pricingcategory.md) sits in the same position as the third. Neither is adjudicated here: whether and when to apply these criteria to already-published columns is a separate decision, and this revision makes no column-level change.

Round 10 (2026-08-02) tested this rule directly, with two sentinels chosen to exercise it: BillingAccountType for the inadmissible branch and PricingCurrency for the admissible one. BillingAccountType was unanimous. PricingCurrency split two to one, because the rule as tested named a level consequence only for the inadmissible branch and left the reader to supply the step from an admissible gate to `Conditional`. The rule no longer has an admissible branch to state: a gate on variance returns Mandatory in every case, so the step the split turned on is gone. Whether the current wording holds PricingCurrency together is work for a later round, and it is the column to watch. The round is recorded in the [blind-rater evidence](feature-level-rubric-blind-rater-evidence.md).

## Why Mandatory is Earned

The rubric's central reframe makes Mandatory a high bar a column earns: the concept exists for every operating model and a value is naturally producible, so no reasonable generator would have it null on every row. Anything a reasonable operating model would leave entirely null is Conditional, gated on the operating model Condition that marks where the concept lives.

The reframe was not the starting position. Earlier drafts treated Mandatory-with-honest-nulls as the safer default because it preserves a stable column set for consumers. Blind-rater testing moved the design: raters applying the earlier text kept re-deriving Conditional for columns whose concept is not universal, and the fallback rules that propped up Mandatory for those columns turned out to be the same gap-filling substitutions the rubric was written to catch. The consumer-stability concern did not disappear; it moved into two guardrails (the definitional-equality versus gap-filling-substitution distinction, and the counterexample test in Tie-Breakers and Defaults) and into an open consumer-side question noted below.

## Why This Revision Narrows to Mandatory and Conditional

An earlier draft covered all four feature levels and the prior question of whether a proposed column belongs in the schema at all. That draft is retained as the alternative, fuller treatment in PR #2507. This revision deliberately narrows to the Mandatory and Conditional criteria, for three reasons:

* **The asymmetry that blocks adoption lives entirely in those two levels.** A generator whose operating model cannot produce a column is harmed by a Mandatory obligation, not by a Recommended one. Fixing the Mandatory-versus-Conditional line delivers the adoption outcome FR 2335 asks for; the remaining levels do not gate it.
* **Only those two criteria currently meet the guideline's own bar.** The bar is that two reviewers applying the text to the same column reach the same level without the author present. The applicability and producibility tests reached that standard in blind-rater testing through round 10, against the pre-restructure text; the [blind-rater evidence](feature-level-rubric-blind-rater-evidence.md) records that no round has yet run against the restructured guideline, and human inter-rater testing remains the planned confirmation. A comparable test for Recommended has not been written: the distinction that would underpin it, between a column a consumer can rely on and one supplied at generator discretion, resists a deterministic statement, and drafting one under time pressure would ship a rule the working group could not apply consistently.
* **The deferred questions are separable.** Deciding which of Mandatory or Conditional an admitted column takes does not depend on how a column earns its place in the schema, nor on what becomes of the two weaker levels. The narrowing therefore costs nothing in the criteria that remain, and the deferred work can proceed on its own timeline.

What the narrowing gives up is real and should be stated plainly: the guideline can no longer be applied end to end to a net-new column proposal, because it presumes the decision to add the column has already been made elsewhere. Until the deferred revision lands, that admission decision stays where it has always been, with the working group's judgment on the proposal.

The narrowing takes no position on the future of the Recommended and Optional levels. The columns holding them are unaffected, and the earlier draft's stance on them is not carried forward here.

## The Applicability Matrix and the Flip Criterion

The companion mechanics guideline defines the data-driven test the principles defer to. Its method is designed and has been dry-run; its calibration has not happened, because the data to calibrate it does not exist yet. The method:

* **The matrix.** Rows are columns under test; columns are representative operating-model profiles, current and prospective (cloud hyperscaler, flat-rate SaaS, usage-based SaaS, PaaS, data center, AI provider, internal IT). Each cell records how that profile would treat that column: **P** (populated), **N** (null), **F** (a value would have to be fabricated), or **?** (unknown).
* **The flip criterion.** A column that is F, or all-N across every row, for more than roughly a third of representative non-cloud profiles is Conditional, gated on the operating model Condition that distinguishes the profiles where it is live. A column that is mostly P with occasional row-level N is Mandatory with Allows nulls = True. Any F cell is treated as disqualifying for the current level, because a fabrication-forcing level is never correct.
* **The threshold is a parameter, not a principle.** The one-third trigger is directional. The designed calibration restates the pattern test as a prevalence share over a census of real generator exports, with the cutoff set from that data. The directional expectation is that one unusual operating model is an exception and two or more distinct operating models are a pattern, but the number is an evidence question.

### What the Dry Run Showed

A full dry run of the matrix and a back-test across the audit columns were performed during design (2026-06-29). The honest status of that run:

* **Only the cloud anchors are measured.** The populated cells for cloud profiles come from real data. Every non-cloud cell that actually drives the flip criterion is operating-model inference (reasoning about what a flat-rate SaaS would populate), because no non-cloud generator's FOCUS export was available cell by cell. The threshold can presently be reasoned but not empirically calibrated.
* **The structural columns are stable.** Region, resource, commitment-discount, and sub-account families sit far from the flip line under any plausible threshold; their levels do not move as the parameter moves.
* **A pricing and usage cluster is threshold-sensitive.** Unit-price columns, the SKU family, and consumed-quantity columns sit near the one-third line, and their levels genuinely depend on where calibration lands. This is why the rubric refuses to settle them by argument and why the Conditional default in Tie-Breakers and Defaults exists.
* **The back-test surfaced real gaps, not just confirmations.** The back-test flagged the splits between cost and price columns under the rubric's earlier derivation-symmetry expectation; the rubric has since replaced symmetry with directional derivation, which levels each column by its own inputs and constrains only the direction (a derived column can be no more present than its sources), so a level split between a cost and its unit price is no longer a gap in itself. Two operating model Conditions the analysis leans on did not exist in the Conditions section at the time: a principals or actor-identification Condition, and any marketplace, reseller, or billing-relationship Condition. The second was previously treated as the boundary of the operating-model abstraction: applicability that turns on which entity issues the invoice, or whether a host provider is exposed to the customer, reads as an ill fit only under a narrowed reading of the operating model. The Glossary counts participating entities, including host providers and invoice issuers, as part of the operating model, so the guideline now routes such applicability to a Condition on the participating entity rather than to a recorded counterexample.

## Design Questions Still Open

These are known and deliberately unresolved; several need stress testing or data before they can be settled:

* **Whether admissibility is a sufficient guard at n = 1.** The level question is settled: Tie-Breakers and Defaults states that an admissible operating model which genuinely lacks the concept makes the column Conditional, and lets the working group set a purported counterexample aside only by recording that it is inadmissible or that it holds the concept after all. A round 10 rater found the earlier exception path unreachable as written, which is what drove the change. What stays open is the gaming concern that path was carrying: one poor implementation becoming the hook that demotes a column everyone else populates. Admissibility is now the only guard against it, and whether it is strong enough is untested.
* **How binding the Conditional default should be.** Tie-Breakers and Defaults sends a boundary column to Conditional, and blind-rater testing has now shown four times that the default gets acknowledged and then skipped: a rater identifies the boundary, then levels the column Mandatory anyway. Round 9 explained why. When the default had an added qualifier making it apply where the answer was "genuinely unclear," it fired inversely with rater confidence, binding the uncertain reader and releasing the confident one, which is the opposite of what a default is for. Restoring the unqualified wording recovered the outcome, but the unqualified wording is itself what the earlier rounds found soft. The open question is whether the default needs strengthening beyond its current form, for instance by stating that a reviewer who identifies a boundary case has thereby met the condition for the default rather than acquiring discretion over it. That is a normative change to how the rule binds, not an editorial one, so it belongs to the working group rather than to a drafting pass.
* **All-null versus fabrication, same treatment or split.** The flip criterion treats a high-N column and an any-F column similarly (both push to Conditional), but they are different harms: a dead column versus a corrupting value. Whether the rule should split is open.
* **The refinement carve-out and display-name columns (deferred).** A necessity test demotes a column that only refines a result another Mandatory column already delivers. Applied to display names that sit under Mandatory identifiers, that reading marks them useful rather than needed; blind-rater testing against the fuller draft produced it in four consecutive rounds, and in two of those it was the majority reading. Whether such a carve-out is meant to reach identifier-name pairs, or only classification hierarchies, needs an explicit decision. This question travels with the deferred admission tests and is not live in the current guideline, which levels a display-name column by applicability like any other.
* **What becomes of Recommended and Optional (deferred).** Whether the two weaker levels are given deterministic criteria, narrowed, or retired is unresolved, as is what happens to the columns that hold them today. Both readings have support: retiring them concentrates the schema on levels a consumer can rely on, while keeping them preserves a defined home for columns that are worth publishing but cannot be required, which matters because data generators tend to emit only what FOCUS has defined. This revision decides neither, and the decision needs its own evidence rather than falling out of the Mandatory-versus-Conditional work.
* **The consumer-side counterpart.** The rubric constrains generators (no padding, no fabrication), and the write-back is its counterpart: whether a Conditional level is complete before the Supported Features that depend on the column account for its absence. An earlier draft stated this as principle 7. The current guideline does not carry it as a principle. What it states instead is the Step 2 Supported Feature floor, which bars `Recommended` and `Optional` for a Directly Dependent Column, and it lists what a Supported Feature does about a column that can be absent as a Deferred Topic belonging to the Supported Features work. What those features should do is still open. Three shapes were put forward in review: a generator-side default, which is the pattern the rubric retires; a consumer-side default defined per feature; and excluding the absent data, which silently reduces coverage and biases cross-generator results. The second is the working assumption, with the per-feature choice belonging to the Supported Features work rather than to this guideline. One refinement the rubric forces: under it the column is absent rather than null, so a feature needs a rule for combining dataset instances rather than per-row null handling. The live first case is [ListCost](../../specification/datasets/cost_and_usage/columns/listcost.md) and [ContractedCost](../../specification/datasets/cost_and_usage/columns/contractedcost.md), which round 9 leveled Conditional unanimously and which Cost Comparison lists among its directly dependent columns. The backlog behind that first case is measurable: as of the 1.5 working draft on 2026-08-02, 24 of the 46 columns leveled Conditional are directly dependent columns of at least one Supported Feature, spanning 10 features. The Step 2 floor is prospective and does not reopen those levels, but they are where the write-back work lands once the per-feature rule is settled.
* **Calibration data.** The prevalence threshold waits on cell-level column-population evidence from real non-cloud generator exports: conformance submissions are the natural source, targeted requests to non-cloud generators the alternative. Until then Tie-Breakers and Defaults governs, and it is expected to govern the whole current release cycle.

## Relationship to the Guideline and the Companion

The guideline covers what is stable and in scope: the operating-model basis, the earned-Mandatory bar, the two axes, the directional derivation principle, the five-step Decision Procedure that applies them, the presence-versus-nullability discriminator, and the tie-breakers and defaults that stand in until calibration. The companion mechanics guideline defines the machinery described here: the matrix, the calibrated threshold, the records, the back-test, and a machine-readable check. The split is deliberate: the principles can be adopted and applied with the tie-breakers and defaults now, and nothing in them changes when calibration replaces those defaults with a measured threshold.
